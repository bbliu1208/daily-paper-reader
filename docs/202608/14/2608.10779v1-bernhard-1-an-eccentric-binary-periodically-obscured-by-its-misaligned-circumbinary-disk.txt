Zhecheng Hu ( 胡哲程) ,1 Wei Zhu ( 祝伟) ,1 Ping Chen ( 陈平) ,2 Richard Post, 3 and Weicheng Zang ( 臧伟呈) 4

ABSTRACT Bernhard-1 is a proposed KH 15D-like circumbinary disk occultation (CBO) system, but its binary nature and disk geometry have not previously been confirmed. We present new optical and near-infrared spectroscopy together with multi-band photometric monitoring of the system. The radial velocities confirm that Bernhard-1 hosts a highly eccentric binary with e = 0 .80 ± 0.09, confirming that the periodic photometric variability arises from occultation by a misaligned circumbinary disk. Joint modeling of the spectra and phase-dependent spectral energy distributions yields pre-main-sequence components with masses of ∼ 1.1 M⊙ and ∼ 0.8 M⊙. Combining stellar isochrones with the measured lithium abundance yields a system age of ∼ 10 Myr. Together with the spatial, astrometric, and metallicity properties of Bernhard-1, this suggests that Bernhard-1 is probably a member of the open cluster Dolidze 42. By combining the RV orbit with a semi-transparent occultation-screen model, we infer a disk–binary mutual inclination of roughly 50 ◦ or 130 ◦, with the degeneracy arising from the unknown disk rotation direction. This geometric method can be applied to any CBO system once radial velocity monitoring yields an orbital solution. The new light curves deviate from earlier model predictions, consistent with ongoing disk precession, while the phase-dependent H α profiles indicate pulsed accretion near periastron. Bernhard-1 therefore joins KH 15D and Bernhard-2 as a rare spectroscopically confirmed CBO system. 

Keywords: Circumstellar disks; Variable stars; Spectroscopy; Pre-main sequence stars 

1. INTRODUCTION Binary stars are common outcomes of star formation, with a multiplicity fraction of nearly 50% among field solar-type stars in the solar neighborhood (D. Raghavan et al. 2010; G. Duchˆ ene & A. Kraus 2013). Planets orbiting both stars in a binary, known as circumbinary planets (CBPs), therefore provide an important test of planet formation and orbital evolution in dynamically complex environments. To date, 17 CBPs have been confirmed, with 14 found by transit (e.g., L. R. Doyle et al. 2011; V. B. Kostov et al. 2020) and the rest by eclipse timing variations (M. Goldberg et al. 2023) or by a dedicated radial velocity survey such as the Binaries

Escorted By Orbiting Planets (BEBOP) survey (D. V. Martin et al. 2019). Of particular interest is the alignment between the bi-nary and planetary orbital planes. Transit detections, however, are biased toward coplanar systems, and all known transiting CBPs have mutual inclinations < 4.5◦

(D. V. Martin & A. H. M. J. Triaud 2014; Z. Chen & D. Kipping 2022). Yet highly misaligned and even po-lar CBPs are predicted to exist, since the polar con-figuration is another stable attractor, especially for ec-centric binaries (e.g., R. G. Martin & S. H. Lubow 2017; J. J. Zanazzi & D. Lai 2018; A. C. Childs & R. G. Martin 2021). Demographic arguments based on the transiting sample suggest that, if giant planets occur with similar frequencies around single and binary stars, the observed yield is consistent with a coplanar CBP population (D. J. Armstrong et al. 2014; D. V. Mar-tin & A. H. M. J. Triaud 2014). However, this infer-arXiv:2608.10779v1 [astro-ph.SR] 11 Aug 2026 2ence remains indirect because highly inclined systems are intrinsically difficult to detect. The true mutual-inclination distribution of CBPs therefore still lacks di-rect observational constraints. Because circumbinary protoplanetary disks define the birth planes of circumbinary planets, their alignment distribution offers a useful proxy for the primordial in-clination distribution of CBPs. Many such disks are observed to be misaligned with eccentric central bina-ries (e.g., K. E. Kearns & W. Herbst 1998; R. K¨ ohler 2011; G. M. Kennedy et al. 2012, 2019; C. Brinch et al. 2016; S. Lacour et al. 2016; M. Fern´ andez-L´ opez et al. 2017; M. A. Kenworthy et al. 2022; Z. Hu et al. 2024). This misalignment appears especially common around binaries with intermediate orbital periods (30–10 5 days; I. Czekala et al. 2019), which are longer than those of the currently known transiting CBP hosts. Misaligned circumbinary disks therefore constrain both the initial conditions of CBP evolution and a binary parameter space largely inaccessible to the present CBP sample. Among misaligned circumbinary disks, the circumbi-nary disk occultation (CBO) systems, of which KH 15D is the prototype, are uniquely valuable (K. E. Kearns & W. Herbst 1998). In this configuration, a misaligned cir-cumbinary disk periodically occults the central binary, producing characteristic flux variations (E. I. Chiang & R. A. Murray-Clay 2004; J. N. Winn et al. 2004, 2006). Decades of photometric follow-up of KH 15D have re-vealed long-term changes in its light curve driven by disk precession, and detailed modeling has constrained the disk–binary mutual inclination to ∼ 15 ◦ and the disk warp to ∼ 10 ◦ (J. N. Winn et al. 2006; M. Poon et al. 2021). Such systems are therefore rare cases in which time-domain photometry, when combined with orbital information, can be converted into geometric constraints on a young circumbinary disk. Large-scale photometric surveys are now rapidly ex-panding the CBO sample, but spectroscopic confirma-tion lags far behind. More than 30 CBO candidates have been proposed from the Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF, E. C. Bellm et al. 2019; F. J. Masci et al. 2019; W. Zhu et al. 2022; Z. Hu et al. 2026), the Optical Gravitational Lensing Experiment (OGLE, A. Udalski et al. 1997; A. Udalski 2003; A. Udalski et al. 2015; M. A. Urbanowicz et al. 2026), All-Sky Automated Survey for Supernovae (ASAS-SN, B. J. Shappee et al. 2014; C. S. Kochanek et al. 2017; R. For´ es-Toribio et al. 2025; N. L. Zakamska et al. 2025) and the VISTA Variables in the Via Lactea survey (VVV, D. Minniti et al. 2010; P. W. Lucas et al. 2024). Yet only one, Bernhard-2, has had its central bi-nary confirmed by the radial velocity (RV) method (Z. Hu et al. 2024), with e ≃ 0.7 and a potentially highly misaligned disk. In this work, we confirm Bernhard-1, which is the other candidate CBO system identified in W. Zhu et al. (2022), based on dedicated spectroscopic and multi-band photometric observations. Located at (20 h20 m55 .22 s, +38 ◦13 ′23 .1′′ ), Bernhard-1 is a 192-day system with r-band magnitudes of 17.30 outside occul-tation and 19.42 during occultation, making it both op-tically fainter and longer-period than Bernhard-2 (W. Zhu et al. 2022). The radial velocities reveal a highly eccentric central binary, confirming that the periodic variability arises from occultation by circumbinary ma-terial. We jointly model the spectra and spectral energy distributions (SEDs) to infer the stellar properties, and we model the light curve with a semi-transparent oc-cultation screen. Combining the screen geometry with the RV orbit yields a rough disk–binary mutual inclina-tion of 50 ◦ or 130 ◦. The light curve also shows long-term changes attributable to disk precession, while the phase-dependent H α profiles indicate pulsed accretion near pericenter passage. All of these features closely resemble the observations of KH 15D and Bernhard-2. The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes our spectroscopic and photometric observations. Sec-tion 3 infers the stellar parameters from the spectra and SEDs. Section 4 models the RV orbit, while Section 5 presents our semi-transparent occultation-screen model. Section 6 discusses the cluster association, lithium age, disk geometry, precession, and pulsed accretion. We summarize our conclusions in Section 7.

STELLAR PARAMETER MODELING 3.1. Spectral Fitting 

We model each spectrum with a single-star template, because one star dominates the observed flux in each observation. Outside occultation, the secondary con-tributes only ∼ 0.16 of the total flux in the wavelength range of the low-resolution GTC spectra, similar to the case of Bernhard-2 (Z. Hu et al. 2024), so we use the GTC/OSIRIS spectra to derive the stellar parameters and RVs of the primary star. The MMT/MMIRS spec-trum, in contrast, was obtained during ingress, when the primary contributes about one-third of the total flux. We therefore use its single-star fit only as a consistency check rather than as an unbiased measurement, noting also that its RV may be distorted by a line-profile ef-fect during partial occultation analogous to the Rossiter-McLaughlin effect (R. A. Rossiter 1924; D. B. McLaugh-lin 1924), which is believed to affect RV measurement of KH 15D (J. N. Winn et al. 2006). We fit the spectra with starfish (I. Czekala et al. 2015), which interpolates synthetic spectral grids and models correlated residuals with a Gaussian Process (GP), reducing the biases in the inferred stellar param-eters caused by imperfect templates and observational systematics. Separate optical and NIR emulators were trained for the GTC and MMT spectra, covering 588– 684 nm and 996–1350 nm, respectively. The underlying grids were generated with iSpec (S. Blanco-Cuaresma 2019), using the SPECTRUM radiative-transfer code (R. O. Gray & C. J. Corbally 1994), ATLAS9 model atmospheres (F. Castelli & R. L. Kurucz 2003), the N. Grevesse & A. J. Sauval (1998) solar abundance scale, and the VALD linelist (T. Ryabchikova et al. 2015). Both grids span Teff =4000–6000 K, log g =3.0–5.0, and [Fe /H] = −1.5 to 0.5, with spacings of 200 K, 0.5 dex, and 0.3 dex, respectively. Microturbulent and macro-turbulent velocities were estimated using the empiri-cal prescriptions implemented in iSpec . Although the young stars like Bernhard-1 may rotate more rapidly, line broadening in our spectra is dominated by the low spectral resolution. As a result, we fix the projected ro-tational velocity to 1.60 km s −1 and the limb-darkening coefficient to 0.6 (D. F. Gray 2005). Because our grids are continuum-flattened, we removed the Planck scaling term from the GP covariance matrix, which is designed for flux-calibrated grids such as the PHOENIX library (e.g., T.-O. Husser et al. 2013). 5

The optical and NIR spectra were fit with slightly different sampling strategies. For the optical spectra, we adopted uniform priors within the grid for Teff and [Fe /H], and a prior on log g from an initial SED fitting (Section 3.2), because low-resolution spectra alone con-strain surface gravity only weakly (M. S. Xiang et al. 2015). We first optimized the third-order Chebyshev baseline, stellar parameters, and GP hyper-parameters with the Nelder-Mead method (J. A. Nelder & R. Mead 1965) implemented in scipy (P. Virtanen et al. 2020) for all the optical spectra. We then fixed the GP hyper-parameters and sampled the posterior around the best fit with emcee for the seven spectra (D. Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013). For the single usable NIR spec-trum, we directly sampled the posterior with free GP hyper-parameters. We adopt the GTC-derived stellar parameters and RVs with additional systematic noise floors to down-stream modeling. As low-resolution spectra are known to suffer from systematic uncertainties, we added noise floors of 100 K in Teff , 0.3 dex in log g, 0.1 dex in metal-licity. An RV noise floor of 1 km/s will be added as a jit-ter term in RV modeling process. These noise values are comparable to the reported uncertainties of high-SNR LAMOST spectra (M. S. Xiang et al. 2015) and previous GTC/OSIRIS stellar spectra (e.g., D. S. Aguado et al. 2021). The best-fit primary parameters, Teff ,1 ≈ 5100 K with the corresponding log g1 and [Fe /H], are used as a prior in the SED fitting, and the RV measurements are listed in Table 1. Following Z. Hu et al. (2024) and Z. Hu et al. (2026), we constrain the physical parameters of both stars by simultaneously fitting the out-of-occultation and in-occultation SEDs, which measure the total flux of the binary and the flux of the secondary, respectively. The SED model is built on the isochrones package (T. D. Morton 2015), which interpolates the MESA Isochrones and Stellar Tracks (MIST, J. Choi et al. 2016; A. Dotter 2016) to predict broadband magnitudes. The magnitude 6of a single star in band B is given by 

mB = f (EEP , log age , [Fe /H] , d, A V ) (1) where EEP is the equivalent evolutionary phase (A. Dotter 2016), d is the distance, and AV is the V -band extinction, for which we adopt the J. A. Cardelli et al. (1989) law with RV = 3.1. Assuming the two stars are coeval, they share all parameters ex-cept their EEPs, giving six free parameters in total: (EEP 1, EEP 2, log age , [Fe /H] , d, A V ). The model out-of-occultation magnitude in each band is the sum of the two stellar fluxes, while the in-occultation magnitude is that of the secondary alone. The SED fitting is performed on the photometry com-piled in W. Zhu et al. (2022), together with the Gaia 

DR3 parallax as a Gaussian prior on the distance. Anoise floor of 0.03 mag is added in quadrature to all photometric uncertainties to account for calibration sys-tematics and imperfections in the stellar models. The WISE W3 and W4 measurements are excluded because they show clear infrared excess. We first locate the maximum-likelihood solution by optimizing the total log-likelihood, and then sample the posterior with emcee 

(D. Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013). Because the SED and spectral analyses constrain com-plementary parameters, we iterated between them to get a converged solution. The log g1 posterior from an ini-tial SED run was adopted as the prior for the GTC spectral fitting as described in Section 3.1, because low-resolution spectra alone constrain it only weakly. The spectroscopic Teff ,1, log g1, and [Fe /H] of the primary, with the systematic noise floors described in Section 3.1, were then applied as priors on the second, final SED run. The best-fit parameters and uncertainties are listed in Table 2, and the corresponding model SEDs are com-pared with the photometry in Figure 2. As an independent consistency check, we compare the derived secondary temperature with the MMIRS spec-trum fitting result. Although the MMIRS spectrum was taken during ingress and is thus contaminated by the primary, which still contributes ∼ 1/3 of the flux, its fit-ted temperature of ∼4400 K is substantially lower than that of the primary and reasonably close to the derived 

Teff ,2 = 4200 ±90 K, supporting the SED decomposition.

Our physical model represents the occulter as astatic, semi-transparent screen projected onto the sky plane. We assume the projected column density varies smoothly across the edge relative to the stellar radius. We approximate the variation in optical depth of the screen edge as an exponential profile 

τ (s) = τ0 exp ( −s/s 0) . (2) Here s0 is the characteristic scale length. The exponen-tial profile in optical depth comes naturally out of an exponential projected column-density profile. We adopt a sky-plane coordinate system centered on the binary center of mass, with the x-axis pointing toward the as-cending node. The screen edge is specified by its perpen-dicular offset d0 from the origin and by its orientation angle θ0, measured from the x-axis in the direction of the projected orbital motion of the primary (see panel c of Figure 3). The edge position d0 is so defined that the optical depth τ0 = 1. For an arbitrary sky-plane position r = ( x, y ), we define the signed distance from the edge as 

s ≡ ˆqθ · r − d0 = x sin θ0 − y cos θ0 − d0, (3) where ˆ qθ = (sin θ0, − cos θ0) is the unit normal to the screen edge. Points with s > 0 lie on the more transpar-ent side of the screen, where τ < 1 and approaches zero far from the edge, whereas points with s < 0 lie deeper inside the screen, where τ > 1. We treat both stars as point sources, as the scale length s0 ≈ 0.033 AU is several times larger than the stellar radii. The total observed flux is 

Ftot = F1 e−τ (s1) + F2 e−τ (s2), (4) where sj is the signed distance of star j from the screen edge, and F1 and F2 are the unobscured fluxes of the two stars in a given band. In practice, F1 and F2 are deter-mined by linear regression for each trial set of screen parameters, so they are profiled out rather than sam-pled. We fix the orbital motion of the binary to the best-fit solution from the RV modeling. Fitting the physical model to the multi-band light curves yields a tightly constrained screen geometry. We fit the ZTF g, r, and the PO r and i photometry from JD 2460775 to 2460975 simultaneously, sampling the poste-rior of ( θ0, d 0, s 0) with emcee (D. Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013) under uniform priors. The best-fit parameters are listed in Table 2, and the best-fit model is compared with the data in panel (b) of Figure 3. As shown by the flux decomposition in panel (d), the occultation depth is dominated by the primary, while the secondary remains nearly unobscured throughout the orbit. For completeness, we also apply the model of J. N. Winn et al. (2006), and conclude that this model does not yield a physically meaningful solution in the case of Bernhard-1. This model describes the occultation of limb-darkened stars, each surrounded by a halo with 9an exponential brightness profile, by an opaque sharp edge. Applying it to Bernhard-1, the model always con-verged to an unrealistic solution in which the halo is far brighter than the central stars, and forcing the halo to be fainter than the star would substantially degrade the goodness of the fit. Because the preferred solution ob-scures an effectively exponential brightness profile with a sharp edge, it is mathematically equivalent to block-ing a point source with an exponential transmission pro-file. We therefore conclude that the J. N. Winn et al. (2006) model qualitatively converges into the same semi-transparent screen model, although it is not exactly the same as the one we have adopted.

DISCUSSION 6.1. Cluster Identification 

To independently constrain the age, metallicity, and distance of Bernhard-1, we investigated whether it is as-sociated with any known open cluster (OC) or moving group (MG). We compared the system with the OC/MG catalog of E. L. Hunt & S. Reffert (2024) by cross-matching it using the Gaia DR3 sky coordinates and proper motions, along with the SED-derived distance that employs the Gaia parallax as a prior. A direct membership check first shows that the Gaia DR3 source ID of Bernhard-1 is not listed as a member of any cataloged OC or MG. We therefore searched for all OC/MGs whose centers lie within 30 ′ of Bernhard-1, yielding six spatial candidates. Of these, only four fall within 10 σ of Bernhard-1 in proper-motion space, ac-counting for both the measurement uncertainty and the intrinsic cluster scatter, and only Dolidze 42 (hereafter D42) lies within the 3 σ region (panel b of Figure 4). The distance provides an independent filter with the same outcome, that only D42 agrees with Bernhard-1 within 3σ (panel c). We use the metallicity as a final sanity check. Gaia RVS metallicities are unavailable for the members of most nearby candidate clusters. Among the candidates considered here, only one D42 member has a Gaia RVS metallicity measurement, and its metallicity is broadly consistent with our GTC and MMT values (panel d). Despite the qualitative agreement between properties of D42 and Bernhard-1, the membership probability re-mains limited by the distance uncertainty. To quan-tify it, we trained a seven-component Gaussian mixture model (GMM) in proper-motion-parallax space, fixing six components to the means and covariances of the member astrometry of each candidate cluster and fitting one additional component to the field-star population. Drawing 10 4 samples from the Bernhard-1 posterior in this space yields a ∼ 30% probability that Bernhard-1 is a D42 member, against ∼ 70% for the field. This mod-est probability is driven almost entirely by the distance uncertainty, which is large compared with the distance scatter of the D42 members. As shown in the follow-ing subsection, the lithium- and SED-derived ages are nonetheless consistent with the age of D42, which inde-pendently supports the association. 6.2. Lithium Abundance and Age Determination 

The GTC/OSIRIS spectra cover the Li I 6708 ˚ A line, which provides an independent age estimate that tests both the youth of Bernhard-1 and its association with D42. Following the procedure adopted for Bernhard-2 (Z. Hu et al. 2024), we measured the lithium abun-dance using the same spectral-synthesis settings as in Section 3.1. We built a grid spanning A(Li) = 0 .0–6 .0 in steps of 0.1 dex and effective resolving power R = 1500– 3500 in steps of 100, over the 668.6–672.6 nm range, with all other stellar parameters fixed to the values in Table 2. During the fit, we interpolate within this grid, multiply the model by a third-order polynomial to ac-count for the local continuum, and include a jitter term to absorb residual systematics. This yields a lithium abundance of A(Li) = 3 .32 ± 0.11, as shown in the left panel of Figure 5. The lithium abundance implies a young age consistent with the pre-main-sequence interpretation of Bernhard-1. We convert the best-fit lithium feature into an equiva-lent width by integrating the flux difference between the best-fit synthetic spectrum and a zero-lithium model, and then infer the age using eagles (R. D. Jeffries et al. 2023). The resulting 1 σ age range is 4–46 Myr, as shown in the right panel of Figure 5. This lithium age is con-sistent with both the SED-derived age ( ∼ 11 Myr) and the age of D42 ( ∼ 8 Myr), reinforcing the cluster asso-ciation inferred in Section 6.1 and confirming the pre-main-sequence nature of the system. 6.3. Mutual Inclination Between the Disk and Binary 

The phenomenological screen parameters ( d0 and θ0)derived from the light curve provide a geometric con-straint on the disk, regardless of which specific part of the disk causes the occultation. Misaligned circumbi-nary disks typically have low eccentricities ( e ≲ 0.1) that decrease further from the central binary (J. L. Small-wood et al. 2022; E. Ragusa et al. 2020). Therefore, a warped circumbinary disk can be conceptualized as a series of concentric circular rings, only with different in-clinations and twists. The occulting edge, which could be the inner edge, outer edge, or even a warped struc-ture, still corresponds to one of these rings. Apply-ing the d0 correction with a fiducial Rring = 3 ab yields a mutual inclination of imut ∼ 50 ◦ or 130 ◦, and the case with imut = 52 ◦ is shown in Figure 6. CONCLUSION Our new spectroscopic and multi-band photometric observations confirm Bernhard-1 as a CBO system, the third after KH 15D and Bernhard-2 (J. A. Johnson et al. 2004; J. N. Winn et al. 2006; Z. Hu et al. 2024). The radial velocities reveal a highly eccentric binary (e = 0 .80 ± 0.09) composed of two pre-main-sequence K dwarfs of ∼ 1.1 M⊙ and ∼ 0.8 M⊙, whose periodic dimming arises from occultation by a misaligned cir-cumbinary disk. The lithium and SED ages both in-dicate a young system of ∼10 Myr. Furthermore, its spatial, astrometric, and metallicity properties are all consistent with the open cluster Dolidze 42, suggesting that Bernhard-1 could be a member of this ∼ 8 Myr cluster. Combining the RV orbit with the occultation-screen geometry yields a rough disk–binary mutual inclination of imut ∼ 50 ◦ or 130 ◦, with the degeneracy arising from the unknown disk rotation direction. This mutual incli-nation is substantially larger than the ∼ 10 ◦ measured for KH 15D, the well-studied prototype of CBO systems. Considering its high eccentricity, Bernhard-1 likely will evolve into a polar aligned configuration. The light curve shows a shorter occultation than was observed around 2020, implying a precession rate of about 0.7 ◦ per binary orbital period, while the phase-dependent H α profiles show enhanced inverse P-Cygni profile near pericenter. All three spectroscopically confirmed CBO systems thus share these two features. The precession is expected, although the differences in rate among systems require dedicated modeling. The persistence of pulsed accretion, on the other hand, may indicate that the general accretion pattern observed in coplanar systems also applies to misaligned circumbi-nary disks. Bernhard-1 nonetheless joins a still small, statistically limited, and yet scientifically valuable sample: to date, only three of more than 30 proposed CBO candidates have been confirmed via spectroscopy. Systematic RV monitoring is required to increase the size of this sam-ple. Such observations will provide constraints on the distribution of primordial disk–binary alignments and, in turn, on the initial conditions governing the forma-tion of circumbinary planets.
