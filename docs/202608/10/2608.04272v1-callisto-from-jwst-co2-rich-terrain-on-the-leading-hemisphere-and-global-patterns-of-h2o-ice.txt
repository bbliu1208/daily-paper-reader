ABSTRACT We present maps of H 2O, CO 2, and a 4 .57 μm spectral feature across Callisto’s surface observed using the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). H 2O ice was mapped by measuring band parameters of the 3 .1 μm Fresnel peak across the leading and trailing hemispheres under a simplified assumption of crystalline ice. We update the CO 2 solid-phase, CO 2 gas, and 4 .57 μm feature band depth maps originally presented in Cartwright et al. (2024) with a new JWST observation of Callisto centered on Valhalla, the largest multi-ring impact basin in the solar system. Our H 2O ice map shows that the Fresnel peak on the trailing hemisphere exhibits a bullseye pattern that is weaker at low latitudes and on the leading hemisphere its strength is associated with impacts. This dichotomy is possibly related to the Jovian magnetospheric plasma impinging on the trailing hemisphere. Our solid-phase CO 2 map reveals an enhancement in the vicinity of the Lofn/Heimdall impact craters, a region that may be the largest reservoir of non-radiolytic CO 2 on Callisto’s surface. The gas-phase CO 2 exhibits a patchy spatial distribution and does not clearly correlate with solid CO 2.

1. INTRODUCTION The Galilean moons of Jupiter—Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto—host an incredible treasury and range of geologic activity. Evidence for endogenic geologic processes including tectonic deformation, volcanism, and surface-interior interactions is readily found on Io (Morabito et al. 1979; de Kleer et al. 2024; Davies et al. 2006), Europa (McCord et al. 1999; Schmidt et al. 2011), and Ganymede (Bagenal et al. 2004), but is largely absent from Callisto (Moore et al. 2004). Although Callisto is excluded from the Laplace resonance that leads to the tidal heating on the inner three moons, Galileo magnetometer results indicate the signature of an induced magnetic field, possibly originating from a saline subsurface ocean (Zimmer 2000; Cochrane et al. 2025; Strack & Saur 2024; Hartkorn & Saur 2017). At the global scale, Callisto’s geology is dominated by large impacts, including exotic multi-ring impact basins of great diameters unseen on any other solar system object (Moore et al. 2004). Callisto’s surface volatile inventory includes H 2O, CO 2, and possibly S-bearing compounds (Hibbitts et al. 2000; McCord et al. 1998; Cartwright et al. 2020; Carlson 1999). These volatiles are accompanied by the largest share of dark material in the Galilean system, as evidenced by Callisto bearing the lowest albedo ( ∼0.2) of the four moons (e.g., see Fig. 4 of King et al. 2025). This dark material is hypothesized to be a thick lag deposit formed by the removal of high-albedo volatiles via continual sublimation (Moore et al. 1999; Spencer 1987; Spencer & Maloney 1984), akin to a similar process observed on comets. Substantial amounts of dust may have accumulated on Callisto’s surface over the age of the solar system, perhaps with some of it originating from the Jovian irregular satellites (Bottke et al. 2013). As such, by virtue of its geologic

2quiescence, Callisto serves as a template for understanding how icy moons evolve under the near exclusive control of exogenic deposition and irradiation processes. Exposed H 2O ice on Callisto and its sibling moons is central to feeding surface chemistry and atmospheric processes. Because the Galilean satellites are embedded in the intense Jovian magnetosphere, H 2O ice on their surfaces is altered by charged particles. Such particles include electrons, protons, and oxygen and sulfur ions of varying charge states (e.g., S ++ ), spanning a wide range of energies from ∼10s eV to 100s of MeV (Cassidy et al. 2010; Bagenal et al. 2015 and references therein). For instance, H 2O ice radiolysis is a fundamental step in the formation of molecular oxygen (Johnson & Quickenden 1997). Molecular oxygen exists in the icy Galilean satellite atmospheres as inferred from UV and optical atomic O emissions (de Kleer et al. 2023; Cunningham et al. 2015; Hall et al. 1995, 1998; Roth et al. 2016), as well as on their surfaces, likely trapped in H 2O ice (Spencer & Calvin 2002; Trumbo et al. 2021; Oza & Johnson 2024; Johnson & Jesser 1997). Additionally, H 2O ice might function as a trapping mechanism for some portion of solid-phase CO 2 on icy satellites (e.g., Cruikshank et al. 2010; Brown et al. 2025). In the near-infrared, the state of H2O ice may be probed through analyses of spectral features in the 1 to 3 μm range. Examples of observations or inferences of H 2O ice on Callisto go back many decades (e.g., Lebofsky 1977; Pollack et al. 1978; Clark & McCord 1980; Pilcher et al. 1972; Morrison & Cruikshank 1973; Fink et al. 1973), and one of the primary sources of information about Callisto’s H 2O ice was the Near-Infrared Mapping Spectrometer (NIMS) instrument onboard the Galileo spacecraft. The NIMS instrument provided spectral measurements in the 0.7–5.2 μm range with a spectral resolving power of R 

∼40–200 (Carlson et al. 1992). Generally, the NIMS Callisto observations showed that the presence of exposed H 2Oice generally tracked with the presence of craters, however the spectra were often noisy due to internal instrument performance and Callisto’s low levels of exposed surface ice ( ∼10 % areal coverage, Hansen & McCord 2004). Beyond H 2O ice, Callisto’s volatile library also retains CO 2, which is notable given that this compound is not stable at the orbit of Jupiter (Watson et al. 1962; Ahrens et al. 2022). Indeed, many surfaces in the Jovian system bear solid CO 2, including the icy Galilean moons (Cartwright et al. 2024; McCord et al. 1998; Hibbitts et al. 2002; Villanueva et al. 2023a; Trumbo & Brown 2023) as well as the irregular satellite (Sharkey et al. 2025) and Trojan populations (Wong et al. 2024). Although pure CO 2 ice as studied in the lab has an asymmetric stretch ( ν3) absorption feature at 4.27 μm (Sandford & Allamandola 1990), measured icy moon CO 2 often presents a shorter wavelength feature closer to 4.25 μm indicative of trapped CO 2, with proposed host materials including both icy (e.g., H 2O) and non-icy substrates (Brown et al. 2025; Ahrens et al. 2022). As such, the origin of Jovian satellite CO 2 may be revealed by placing the spatial distribution of the CO 2 band shape and center in context with geologic features. For example, the enhanced abundance of Europa’s surficial CO 2 near chaos terrains, combined with an analysis of the ν3 band shape, strongly suggests a link to recent, internal sources (Trumbo & Brown 2023; Villanueva et al. 2023a). On Callisto and Ganymede, their surface CO 2 further acts to feed their CO 2 atmospheres (Carlson 1999; Cartwright et al. 2024; Bockel´ ee-Morvan et al. 2024a). However, the balance of mechanisms that promote the release of CO 2 from the surface into the atmosphere remains uncertain. As demonstrated by Cartwright et al. (2024), the peak column densities of CO 2 gas on Callisto do not align with the peak CO 2 surface abundance as inferred from band depth maps, nor do they align with the equatorial afternoon where the surface temperature is warmest. More complete coverage of the CO 2 gas would help in determining how universal this patchiness is across Callisto’s surface. At present, studies of H 2O and the chemistry of volatiles in the Galilean system are being greatly advanced by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). As an infrared telescope, JWST is sensitive to the ro-vibrational absorption and emission features of solid- and gas-phase volatiles that dominate the surface compositions of these moons. With the incredible spatial resolution of JWST, the location of these volatiles can be tethered to geologic maps of the surface made from spacecraft data, such that their origins (either endogenic or exogenic) can be evaluated. For example, SO gas has been observed directly above an active volcano on Io (de Pater et al. 2023), and observations of Ganymede show in great detail how the moon’s intrinsic magnetic field promotes the collection of select volatiles near the poles (Trumbo et al. 2023; Bockel´ ee-Morvan et al. 2024a). The first published analysis of Callisto using JWST’s Near Infrared Spectrograph instrument (NIRSpec) focused on one observation each of the leading and trailing hemispheres (Cartwright et al. 2024). This analysis offered a comprehensive look at Callisto’s abundant surface CO 2, and included the first detection of CO 2 gas on Callisto since the Galileo mission in the 1990s (Carlson 1999). Here, we analyze a third JWST observation of Callisto centered on its largest impact basin, Valhalla. Using these data, we provide new full-coverage maps of H 2O ice using the 3.1 μm Fresnel reflectance peak, as well as updated analysis of CO 2 and a possible 4 .57 μm organics feature explored in Cartwright et al. (2024). Within the existing JWST data set for Callisto, the 3.1 μm Fresnel peak is the sole H 2O3ice feature that is fully sampled across wavelength space. Although this feature is sensitive to various attributes of water ice (e.g., grain size, temperature), determining how its major properties such as band depth and band center vary across the disk offers a simplified overview of crystalline H 2O ice processes on Callisto. The organization of this paper is as follows: Section 2 describes the reduction of the JWST NIRSpec data and parameter extraction for the spectral features. Section 3 describes the results of mapping the Fresnel Peak across Callisto, as well as the maps of CO 2 and the 4 .57 μm feature. Section 4 offers our interpretation of these features, including how localized enhancements do/do not correlate with local geology. We also place these new Callisto results in context with those obtained for the other icy Galilean moons. Section 5 summarizes the conclusions of this work.

METHODS 2.1. Observations and Data Reduction 

Observations of Callisto were obtained using the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), located at the L2 Lagrange point of the Sun-Earth system (Gardner et al. 2023). With its 6.5 m primary mirror, excellent sensitivity, and high spatial resolution, JWST enables detailed mapping of spectral features on solar system targets. We used the NIRSpec instrument (Jakobsen et al. 2022) as part of General Observer program 2060 (P.I. Cartwright), which observed Callisto on 2022 November 15 and 25 (previously analyzed by Cartwright et al. 2024) and 2023 September 20 (newly presented in this work). The respective sub-observer longitudes of these observations were 279 ◦W, 137 ◦W, and 56 ◦W; these viewing geometries sampled Callisto’s trailing hemisphere, the Asgard impact basin, and the Valhalla impact basin, respectively. The position of Callisto relative to Jupiter, and relative to Jupiter’s centrifugal equator, are shown in Fig. 1. Data were collected using NIRSpec’s integral field unit (IFU) that has a 3 ′′ × 3′′ field of view, and a pixel size of 0.1 ′′ . The G395H grating was used in all three observations, spanning 2.85–5.35 μm with an average resolving power R ∼ 2700. Each observation was acquired via a set of four 32 s exposures which each sampled different parts of the detector (four dithers), using the NRSRAPID readout mode. The data were downloaded from the Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes (MAST) at the Space Telescope Science Institute, and they can be accessed via https://doi.org/10.17909/e61x-e585. The JWST pipeline versions used for each observation were: 1.12.5 (Sep 20), 1.7.2 (Nov 15), and 1.8.2 (Nov 25). The Calibration References Data System files used for data taken on Nov 15, Nov 25, and Sep 20 respectively are: jwst_1015.pmap , jwst_1017.pmap , and 

jwst_1169.pmap . Additional custom codes were developed to combine dithered frames and remove bad pixels. The four dithers were georeferenced to Callisto’s disk and then median combined, which assisted in removing abnormal pixels. At each spatial pixel (spaxel), we distinguished between reflectance and thermal emission components by applying a two-component model: a realistic solar model for reflected light and a Planck function for thermal radiation. We generated the solar model using the Planetary Spectrum Generator (PSG; Villanueva et al. 2018, 2022), which adjusts for Doppler shifts, uses the high-fidelity ACE solar spectrum (Hase et al. 2010) to account for solar Fraunhofer lines, and employs the Kurucz (2005) model to reproduce the continuum intensity. After subtracting the thermal component, we calculated the reflectance spectrum for each spaxel by dividing the calibrated flux values by a solar model. This model was scaled according to the spaxel’s projected spatial dimensions and corrected for the Sun-Callisto and JWST-Callisto distances at the observation times. This methodology parallels that used in previous analyses of NIRSpec IFU data for Europa (Villanueva et al. 2023a), Callisto (Cartwright et al. 2024), and Enceladus (Villanueva et al. 2023b). 2.2. Band Parameter Analysis Retrieval of solid-state band parameters: After the NIRSpec cubes were thermally-corrected and dither-averaged, we mapped the spatial distribution of spectral features of interest. First, we registered the center point of Callisto’s disk and determined the range of pixels that fall within the projected radius of Callisto at the time of each observation. After spatial registration, we proceeded to map the 4.25 μm CO 2 feature, the 3 .1 μm H 2O ice Fresnel peak, and a 4 .57 μm absorption feature. For these fits, we used the Python package lmfit to retrieve band parameters and statistics (Newville et al. 2025). For the 4.25 μm CO 2 feature, we simultaneously fit a two-Gaussian absorption profile and a linear continuum, while for the 4 .57 μm band we used a single Gaussian plus a linear continuum. The maximum absorption depth for each feature was divided by the local continuum value to retrieve the fractional band depth. For the 3 .1 μm Fresnel reflectance peak, we fit a three-component model that consisted of a 1) linear continuum from 3.00 to 3.30 μm, 2) a Gaussian profile centered near 3.1 μm, and 3) a second Gaussian profile centered near 3.2 μm. We initially tested the Fresnel peak fits with a single Gaussian at 3 .1 μm, however the need for a smaller, secondary component was motivated by structure apparent in the residuals. We note this approach differs from the Fresnel peak band measurement techniques used for Ganymede NIRSpec data by (Bockel´ ee-Morvan et al. 2024b; Trumbo et al. 2023) and for Europa (Cartwright et al. 2025), for which the reported band areas are normalized by the underlying continuum. Retrieval of gaseous CO 2: Although Callisto’s spectral profile around 4.25 μm is dominated by absorption from the solid-phase CO 2, weak gaseous features can be extracted from this same wavelength range via a careful treatment of the continuum. This procedure for JWST NIRSpec observations was first demonstrated at Callisto (Cartwright et al. 2024), and has since been applied to Ganymede (Bockel´ ee-Morvan et al. 2024a). Here, we reiterate the primary details of this extraction. Excited molecules of CO 2 gas produce a fluorescent emission band in the 4.2 to 4.3 μm spectral range. This band is composed of two groups of emission lines called the P and R branches that correspond to different rotational quantum number changed associated with the transitions (Bosman et al. 2017) . These narrow emission lines that make up these branches are resolvable at the resolution of NIRSpec’s G395H grating (R ∼ 3000 at 4.25 μm). These lines are separated from the solid-phase CO 2 absorption by generating a smoothed Callisto spectrum between 4.2 and 4.3 μm (R = 1000) that only retains the shape of the solid-phase profile. Next, the residuals containing the gaseous emission lines were cross-correlated to synthetic spectra of varying CO 2 concentrations generated by PSG. This line-of-sight was not corrected for the observing geometry (i.e., incidence and emission angles). For our PSG model, we assume that the excitation process is photon-dominated, rather than electron-dominated. An assessment of CO 2 electron excitation, employing an electron population comparable to that used for analyzing auroral emissions (Cunningham et al. 2015; de Kleer et al. 2023), indicates minimal contributions ( <0.1 %), reinforcing solar-pumped fluorescence as the primary excitation mechanism at Callisto. The observation rms and χ2 statistics between the finalized continuum model and the data informed the 1 σ uncertainties for the resulting CO 2 column density estimates. 6

RESULTS 3.1. Variation in the Fresnel Peak across Callisto 

The band area maps of Callisto’s Fresnel peak as presented in Fig. 2 demonstrate that crystalline H 2O ice is not homogeneous across the moon. Instead, this H 2O ice feature exhibits leading vs. trailing hemisphere differences, as well as localized enhancements. To complement the Fresnel peak maps, Fig. 3 shows sample spectra from the three observations to highlight the improved spectral resolution of JWST NIRSpec compared to Galileo NIMS. 

Leading vs. Trailing Hemisphere : The Fresnel peak is clearly different on the trailing hemisphere in both strength and spatial distribution compared to the leading hemisphere (Fig. 2). We find band areas are larger on Callisto’s leading hemisphere. The average trailing hemisphere band area is ∼1.9 × 10 −4 μm, while typical values for the Asgard and Valhalla observations are ∼1.2 and ∼1.3 times this value, respectively (Fig. 2). Additionally, the trailing hemisphere exhibits a distinct bullseye pattern with weaker band strengths at low latitudes, and increasing band strengths at high latitudes and toward the anti-Jovian and sub-Jovian limbs. The Asgard and Valhalla observations do not exhibit this bullseye pattern. Instead, the largest band areas on the two leading hemisphere observations are generally associated with large impacts. 

Localized Enhancements on Asgard and Valhalla : For the Asgard-centered observation, there are two main localized regions of elevated band areas. One is centered near the Asgard impact basin at 32 ◦N, 140 ◦W. A representative value for this area is ∼3.7 × 10 −4 μm, which is ∼1.4 times greater than neighboring pixels at similar latitudes. Another smaller (D ∼610 km) multi-ring basin, Utgard, is just north of Asgard’s center at 45 ◦N, 134 ◦W and may also be contributing to this same group of elevated band strength. The second area of elevated Fresnel peak strength in this observing geometry is in the southern hemisphere, around the sub-observer longitude. Beyond these two regional enhancements, the Fresnel band area measurements across the Asgard-centered observation are fairly uniform. Similarly, we find additional enhancements of the Fresnel peak on the Valhalla-centered observation. The dominant feature in the center of Callisto’s disk for this observation is the increased band area near the center of Valhalla, located at 14 ◦N, 56 ◦W. The band areas of pixels near the center are ∼4 × 10 −4 μm. In the south, we find larger swaths of higher band areas, including one region that appears co-located with Lofn near −56 ◦N, 22 ◦W. The corresponding reflectance spectra for these regions are shown in Fig. 3, which illustrates the more pronounced Fresnel peak structure associated with the impact basins, especially compared to the trailing hemisphere. Beyond these broad enhancements of the Fresnel peak area, we also identify a region east of Valhalla between ( ∼0◦–30 ◦N, 0 ◦–30 ◦W) that bears generally weaker band areas than the pixels to the west of Valhalla. The Fresnel peak areas in this region are ∼0.5 × lower than the Valhalla-observation full-disk average. 3.2. The 4.25 μm CO 2 Feature across Callisto 

In Fig. 4, we present an updated map of the 4.25 μm solid CO 2 solid feature across Callisto that includes the Valhalla impact basin. The disk-averaged CO 2 band depth is ∼19 %, while the enhanced pixels near 0 ◦W and −45 ◦Nare closer to ∼25 %. These CO 2-rich pixels are geographically near Callisto’s most prominent southern hemisphere impact features, mainly Lofn and Heimdall ( −63 ◦N, 357 ◦W). In contrast to the Fresnel peak data, the solid-phase CO 2 distribution around Valhalla appears more diffuse. In addition to detecting solid-phase CO 2 across the Valhalla-centered hemisphere, Fig. 5 shows the detection of gas phase CO 2. The characteristic sawtooth pattern of the ν3 fundamental vibration of gaseous CO 2 is seen in the residuals (left panel of Fig. 5). As seen in the right panel of Fig. 5, the NIRSpec spaxels with the highest column densities ( >1.1 × 10 19 m−2) are in the northern hemisphere, within/near Valhalla. We report the mean column densities and corresponding standard deviations of CO 2 for the trailing, Asgard-, and Valhalla-centered observations of (0 .5 ± 0.2) × 10 −19 , (0 .5 ± 0.2) × 10 −19 , and (0 .6 ± 0.3) × 10 −19 m−2, respectively. 3.3. The Geologic Context of Callisto’s 3.1 μm Fresnel Peak 

In Section 3, we presented a high-sensitivity, comprehensive map of Callisto’s 3 .1 μm Fresnel feature. Because these Fresnel peaks are generated by the first reflection of light off an H 2O crystal (e.g., Hansen & McCord 2004 and references therein), these maps can be interpreted as an approximation of the areal exposure of H 2O ice on Callisto’s surface. However, it is important to emphasize the intensity, shape, and band center of the Fresnel peak are functions of multiple factors including temperature, grain size, and crystal structure (i.e., crystalline vs. amorphous, see Stephan et al. 2020; Hansen & McCord 2004; Mastrapa et al. 2009). It is beyond the scope of the current work to include these factors in our analysis, as it would require detailed laboratory experiments and robust radiative transfer modeling. As such, we publish these Fresnel peak area maps as a “first-pass” at Callisto’s H 2O ice distribution as observed by JWST, much like the approach taken for the JWST Ganymede Fresnel peak maps by Bockel´ ee-Morvan et al. (2024b). These “crystalline” results are complemented by recent work by Cartwright et al. (2025) on analyzing the state of H 2Oice across the surface of Europa using JWST. The Fresnel maps presented here represent a step forward in quality compared to the prior Galileo NIMS dataset. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the better resolution of JWST allows for a higher fidelity extraction of features compared to the NIMS data. Because the 3 .1 μm signal in individual NIMS pixels was weak, Hansen & McCord (2004) did not present a global map of this feature, but instead reported averages of icy pixels from five large regions. Other maps of H2O spectral features using NIMS data include those by Hibbitts et al. (2000); McCord et al. (1998); Stephan et al. (2020). In this work, we primarily use the 1.48 μm water ice band depth map in Hibbitts et al. (2000) as a point of reference, as the band depths are presented as a global map, allowing for a straightforward comparison between our data and the Callisto geologic map (Greeley et al. 2000).

For these features, the pixels with the largest Fresnel peaks appear to be contained to the center regions of the craters, with a drop off in strength toward the outer rims. This is mirrored in the albedo maps, which show the brightest material of these craters is retained in the impact centers. Supporting these JWST results are millimeter/submillimeter observations of Callisto using ALMA, which show the Valhalla impact region is co-located with a thermal cold spot discernible in model residuals spanning 3–0.87 mm (Camarca et al. 2025, 2023). This cold spot may be explained if there is higher thermal inertia material present that suppresses daytime temperatures in the crater center, and/or lower emissivity materials, both of which are consistent with a higher abundance of fresh, exposed H 2O ice. Moreover, the dimensions of the JWST NIRSpec pixel are comparable to the ALMA beamsize for the Callisto observations (within a factor of ∼2.5), and we find the spatial extent of the largest Fresnel peaks in this area to be comparable to the size of the cold spot in the ALMA data (of order <0.3′′ ). The large-impact enhancement in the Fresnel peak intensity is accompanied by smaller geologic units with similar levels of enhancement. No obvious ice migration : Another application of the Fresnel peak map is to evaluate an icy moon for evidence of the migration of ice crystals. As reported by Bockel´ ee-Morvan et al. (2024b), the strength of the Fresnel peak on Ganymede’s trailing hemisphere appears to favor the morning limb. Such a distribution could be explained if the morning limb excess represents an accumulation of ice particles that have yet to be sublimated away in the daytime heat. In this work, we do not recover obvious evidence for a morning sublimation front on Callisto (Fig. 2). There is potential evidence for the afternoon limb on the trailing hemisphere bearing elevated Fresnel band areas. If this limb brightening was only controlled by geology, that unit would be located near 210 ◦W, and we do not recover a similar limb brightening for this longitude at the morning limb of the Asgard-centered observation. However, because the strength of Callisto’s Fresnel peak is much weaker than those on Ganymede, pixels near the disk edge must be interpreted with greater caution. Therefore, we do not claim evidence of ice migration in this work. 

Ice Crystallinity on Callisto: One of the useful diagnostics that can be derived from analysis of the Fresnel peak is whether the crystal is amorphous or crystalline. At icy Galilean satellite surface temperatures, the Fresnel peak is broad and weak for amorphous ice, and strong and structured for crystalline ice (Hansen & McCord 2004). The identification of amorphous ice on the Galilean satellites is of interest given the dependence of ice crystallization on factors including temperature and the radiation environment. For example, Jenniskens & Blake (1996) show that for a modest change in temperature from ∼105–125 K, the change in the crystallization timescale is a factor of 10 5 faster. The laboratory work by Jenniskens & Blake (1996) points to thermal recrystallization timescales for 150 K temperatures of order 10 seconds, with timescales under 1 minute for this same temperature including porosity-dependent measurements by Mitchell et al. (2017). The paradigm established by analyses of the NIMS data is that radiation works quickly to produce amorphous ice on Europa, with some available on Ganymede, while thermal crystallization dominates at Callisto (Hansen & McCord 2004). The presence of amorphous ice at Ganymede’s north pole was recovered by Bockel´ ee-Morvan et al. (2024b) using JWST NIRSpec. In the Ganymede data, the Fresnel peak is clearly broad with a lower band center reported at 3.05 μm. Laboratory work by Stephan et al. (2020) demonstrates that the band center of the Fresnel peak can shift closer to 3.09 μm in 150 K crystalline ice, which is broadly consistent with our band center measurements (Fig. 2). 

Interpretation of Leading vs. Trailing: Our finding that Fresnel peak band areas are smaller on the trailing hemi-sphere than on the leading hemisphere is consistent with the trend observed on Callisto’s neighbor, Ganymede (Bockel´ ee-Morvan et al. 2024b). This is apparent by comparing example leading/trailing hemisphere spaxels here (Fig. 3), as well as in the disk-integrated leading/trailing spectra presented in Fig. 4 in Cartwright et al. (2024) with Fig. 4 of Bockel´ ee-Morvan et al. (2024b). The contrast between Callisto’s average leading/trailing hemisphere band areas as measured here ( ∼1.4) is much lower than the contrast of >10 reported for Ganymede. As summarized in Bockel´ ee-Morvan et al. (2024b), the Fresnel peak may be strengthened on the leading hemisphere of Ganymede due to more ions sputtering fresh ice, as well as the effect of increased meteorite flux excavating the ice. For Callisto, the distribution of sputtering ions and electrons likely affects surface processing, albeit to a lesser degree than its sibling Galilean moons (e.g., Cooper et al. 2001; Johnson et al. 2004). However, direct comparisons between Ganymede and Callisto are complicated by the former’s internal magnetic field. Therefore, while Callisto’s muted leading/trailing Fresnel peak asymmetry may partly reflect weakened energetic particle processes, further work is needed to clarify which aspects of the Jovian plasma environment are most relevant to the 3.1 μm feature specifically. Notably, we recover a bullseye pattern, in which the Fresnel peak band area is smaller at the disk center and larger toward the edge, on Callisto’s trailing hemisphere. A similar trend is discernible in maps of the 1.48 μm water ice band on Callisto in Hibbitts et al. (2000). Importantly, a trailing hemisphere Fresnel bullseye is not unique to Callisto, as the JWST map of this feature on Ganymede exhibits the same behavior (Bockel´ ee-Morvan et al. 2024b). One possible explanation for the bullseye pattern is temperature: laboratory work by Stephan et al. (2021a) demonstrates that the intensity of the Fresnel peak decreases with increasing temperature, which would predict a weaker equatorial Fresnel peak on Callisto. However, if temperature were the primary factor varying the Fresnel peak, we would expect weak equatorial band areas in the Asgard- and Valhalla-centered observations, which we do not see. Grain size is another factor to consider; the same laboratory data demonstrate that increasing the grain size of H 2O ice increases the Fresnel peak intensity across the 70–150 K range for grains of order 70–1300 μm (Stephan et al. 2021a). However, a detailed analysis of NIMS data by Stephan et al. (2020) using the 2 μm and 1.5 μm band features finds that Callisto’s trailing hemisphere has an apparent bullseye pattern in grain size, with larger grains at the equator and smaller grains at the poles. CO 2 and exposed H 2O ice on the trailing hemisphere: Earlier in the discussion section, we highlighted that the trailing hemisphere Fresnel peak distribution appears to be an “inverted” bullseye pattern, with depletion at the disk center (Fig. 2). Nevertheless, while radiolytic generation of CO 2 from H2O could be a relevant factor in understanding Callisto’s trailing hemisphere Fresnel peaks, the same is not obvious for Ganymede. Gas-phase CO 2: The original detection of CO 2 gas at Callisto was reported by Carlson (1999), who used an off-limb scan from Galileo during its C10 flyby. Carlson (1999) inferred a column density of 8 × 10 18 m−2, and proposed that the volatility and mobility of CO 2 indicates it should be globally present on Callisto. These major attributes of the atmosphere were recently confirmed using new JWST observations. As determined by Cartwright et al. (2024), CO 2 gas is present across both Callisto’s leading and trailing hemispheres, and the retrieved column densities 0.4– 1.0 × 10 19 m−2, are similar to those reported by Galileo. The fact that the column densities in the JWST data were substantially weaker off the disk supports Carlson (1999)’s conclusion that CO 2 gas is retained close to the surface (<40 km), an unsurprising result given Callisto’s large mass. Our work adds a third JWST detection of CO 2 gas on Callisto, now on the Valhalla-centered hemisphere (Fig. 5, 9). Our reported column densities range from ∼0.4– 1.4 × 10 19 m−2, and are comparable to the previous Cartwright et al. (2024) values for the trailing and Asgard-centered hemisphere. We note that, given the size of JWST’s spatial footprint at the distance of Callisto, we do not attempt an exact measure of the scale height in this work. To put the retrieved CO 2 column densities in context, the inferred abundances of two other main species in Callisto’s atmosphere are O 2 ∼ 4 × 10 19 m−2 and H 2∼0 × 10 18 m−2. The value for O 2 was inferred from excited atomic oxygen emissions (Cunningham et al. 2015; de Kleer et al. 2023), and the constraint for H 2∼ 0 × 10 18 m−2 was modeled by (Carberry Mogan et al. 2022) based on coronal H emission observed by Roth et al. (2017). Altogether, Callisto’s CO 2 is seemingly not the dominant atmospheric species, but is at most about an order of magnitude less abundant than O 2 (and potentially only a few times less). Being the heaviest among the main species (O 2, H 2), CO 2 may be most relevant near the surface and as collisional partner for other molecules. Beyond these global results, the variation of CO 2 gas across the disk in context with the solid-phase CO 2 reveals a spatial pattern for which the origin is not yet clear. As shown in Fig. 9 and first highlighted by Cartwright et al. (2024), CO 2 gas across Callisto is patchy. Across the observations, the position of diffuse gas patches does not correspond to the location of deepest 4.25 μm CO 2 band depths, nor does there seem to be a correlation with the warmest regions, 15 on the afternoon side of the subsolar point (Cartwright et al. 2024). A study of CO 2 gas on Ganymede yielded a similar general result, in that reported peak CO 2 column densities do not overlap with the warm, equatorial regions nor with the largest CO 2 band depths (Bockel´ ee-Morvan et al. 2024a). For Ganymede, the region of highest CO 2

column densities is congregated in the north polar region on the leading hemisphere, while for Callisto we find that broadly the terrain around Valhalla is co-located with Callisto’s elevated column densities (Fig. 9). If the Valhalla region is a source of gaseous CO 2 on Callisto’s leading hemisphere, an explanation is needed to explain why the Lofn/Heimdall region is seemingly insignificant in terms of CO 2 gas despite being the primary solid CO 2 reservoir on this face of the moon. One explanation might be that the Lofn/Heimdall region is at a geographic disadvantage with regard to participating in the processes expected to drive the transfer of CO 2 to the atmosphere from the surface–such as sublimation, which is more relevant at equatorial latitudes. However, this does not explain why peak CO 2 gas at Callisto is observed at high ( ≳30 ◦) latitudes on both hemispheres. The role that Callisto’s thermal properties play in organizing the CO 2 gas across the surface remains unclear in light of this new Valhalla-centered observation. Camarca et al. (2025) suggested the trailing hemisphere CO 2 gas peak in Cartwright et al. (2024) might be related to a cold spot observed in the 3 mm trailing hemisphere residuals. Alternatively, Cartwright et al. (2024) suggested that the warm, sub-Valhalla region detected in the ALMA residuals might promote sublimation of CO 2

in those regions. The release mechanism of CO 2 is likely complicated, much like the CO 2 gas on its sister moon, Ganymede (Bockel´ ee-Morvan et al. 2024a). Of note, these observations are only “snapshots” of the atmosphere—if CO 2 gas is mobile across Callisto, then our ability to diagnose whether the gas is connected to specific terrain or not is limited. 4.3. The 4.57 μm Feature and Organics on Callisto 

The existence of CN-bearing material on Callisto was proposed over 30 years ago at the discovery of the 4 .57 μmfeature in Galileo data (McCord et al. 1997). Subsequent observations (Cartwright et al. 2020), including those using JWST (Cartwright et al. 2024), continue to support CN as a candidate for the 4 .57 μm absorption. If this assignment holds, this absorption may offer a rare glimpse into nitrogen-based chemistry in the Galilean moon system, and may be comparable to the 4.62 μm band detected in astrophysical ices. First observed in a protostar by Soifer et al. (1979), the 4.62 μm feature was later attributed to the OCN − ion by Grim & Greenberg (1987). From the discovery of Callisto’s 4.57 μm band, it was suggested that the responsible compound may have a corresponding feature in interstellar ice spectra (McCord et al. 1997). Many decades of laboratory work confirm the relationship of OCN to the 4.62 μmfeature (D’Hendecourt et al. 1986; Demyk et al. 1998; Hudson et al. 2001; Gerakines et al. 2025). Today, the catalog of OCN detections includes young stellar objects (Van Broekhuizen et al. 2005), and molecular clouds (McClure et al. 2023), and even on trans-Neptunian objects (Cryan et al. 2025). By contrast, detections of bands near 4 .57 μm on icy moons are not common. For example, in the Saturnian system, a narrow band near 4.53–4.55 μm was reported in Cassini VIMS observations of Phoebe (Coradini et al. 2008), however it was not recovered in recent JWST observations of this object (Belyakov & Brown 2025). A weak 4 .57 μm absorption was reported in Ganymede NIMS observations (McCord et al. 1997), however a subsequent detection was not reported in JWST observations (Bockel´ ee-Morvan et al. 2024b) nor in the Juno close fly-by observations using the Jovian Infrared Auroral Mapper instrument (Tosi et al. 2024). The widespread detection of the 4 .57 μm feature on Callisto is clearly unique, and the updated JWST results here offer useful context in developing a working hypothesis for its origin. Previous work has highlighted the potential role of Jovian irregular satellites in delivering CN-bearing dust to Callisto (Cartwright et al. 2024, 2020). There are a few lines of spatial evidence that may be consistent with this hypothesis. First, the primary global attribute of the strength of Callisto’s 4 .57 μm is deeper band depths on the leading hemisphere (Cartwright et al. 2020, 2024). Irregular satellite dust is generally expected to primarily fall on Callisto’s leading hemisphere (Bottke et al. 2013; Chen et al. 2024), which is consistent with a proposed relationship between the 4 .57 μm feature and the irregular satellite population. Second, Davis et al. (2025) proposes that the muted 4.57 μm band depths observed in Asgard and at the edge of Valhalla impact basins in Cartwright et al. (2024) might even be evidence of a “timestamp” on the dust delivery. Under this paradigm, the rapid accretion of Jovian irregular satellite dust in the early stages of Callisto’s surface history (Bottke et al. 2013) was locally interrupted by the large impact basins. This work, which includes a more favorable viewing geometry of Valhalla, suggests that both this basin and Asgard could be depleted in the 4 .57 μm absorption (Fig. 6). 16 While the distribution of Callisto’s 4 .57 μm band might be consistent with delivery of CN-bearing dust from the Jovian irregular satellites, this idea can be further evaluated with new compositional data. Using JWST NIRSpec, Sharkey et al. (2025) procured 0.7–5.2 μm reflectance spectra of eight Jovian irregulars, including three members from the dominant satellite family by mass, the Himalia family. Sharkey et al. (2025) report the surveyed Jovian irregulars do not possess a 4 .57 μm absorption feature. This result suggests that the observed Jovian irregular satellite families are not delivering CN-bearing dust “as is” to Callisto. However, Sharkey et al. (2025) found that some irregular satellites, including Himalia, do possess absorption features at 2.7 and 3.05 μm consistent with the presence of ammoniated phyllosilicates. As such, Sharkey et al. (2025) suggest that perhaps the Jovian irregulars are pollinating Callisto with N-bearing minerals that react with C-rich material on its surface to yield CN-bearing products. It is useful to consider what material is providing the C, and under what conditions. In our study, we find the only link between the 4 .57 μmfeature and CO 2 is a modest (r = –0.61), inverse relationship on the trailing hemisphere (Fig. 7). Because the CO 2

on Callisto’s trailing hemisphere may be radiolytically generated, our result suggests the trailing hemisphere particle environment might be hostile to certain reactions between C-rich material and N-bearing material. Clearly, much work remains to evaluate the origin of the 4 .57 μm band on Callisto. Better hypothesis testing will hinge on laboratory research to study C and N chemistry using temperature and radiation conditions appropriate for an icy moon like Callisto.

CONCLUSIONS The distribution of surface volatiles on Callisto’s leading hemisphere was examined using the NIRSpec instrument onboard JWST. Here, we presented 1) the first global, high-resolution, high-sensitivity map of the water ice Fresnel reflection peak at 3 .1 μm across Callisto’s entire surface, and 2) a new map of spectral features at 4.25 μm (CO 2) and 4.57 μm on a Valhalla-centered viewing geometry. For CO 2, we mapped both the solid-phase distribution as well as the gaseous-phase column densities. From these data products, we concluded the following: — The strength of Callisto’s 3 .1 μm Fresnel peak tracks primarily with geology (i.e., large impacts) on the leading hemisphere. Valhalla, Asgard, and Lofn/Heimdall are all terrains with increased Fresnel band areas. This finding is consistent with their high albedoes and previously measured H 2O ice content from the NIMS instrument onboard 

Galileo (Moore et al. 2004; Hibbitts et al. 2000). On the trailing hemisphere, the Fresnel peak exhibits an “inverted bullseye” pattern, with the band areas being weaker toward the more equatorial latitudes. — The greatest abundance of CO 2 on Callisto’s leading hemisphere is co-located with the Lofn/Heimdall impact basins. The shape and band center of the CO 2 spectral feature in this region is comparable to that in the Valhalla impact basin, suggesting the two impacts host their CO 2 in similar (icy) material. Lofn/Heimdall may host the largest share of non-radiolytic CO 2 on Callisto. It is possible that CO 2 in the Lofn/Heimdall region has remained enriched relative to the rest of the leading hemisphere given its location at high latitudes, where mechanisms that would otherwise release volatiles from the surface—such as solar insolation—are weakened. The thermal properties of the region as inferred from ALMA observations (Camarca et al. 2023, 2025) may further reinforce the retention of CO 2 in this area through lowering peak surface temperature. Fortunately, Lofn and Heimdall have been identified as “Regions of Interest” on Callisto for ESA’s Juice (Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer) mission and its JANUS (Jovis Amorum ac Natorum Undique Scrutator) camera will image this region at ∼3–4 km per pixel, and the visible and infrared Moons And Jupiter Imaging Spectrometer (MAJIS) will achieve 30 m/pixel resolution data (Stephan et al. 2021b). — On the trailing hemisphere, we identify that the band depth of the solid-phase CO 2 is anti-correlated with the strength of the Fresnel peak band area. The regions of highest CO 2 absorption at the low latitudes centered on the trailing hemisphere are the regions of lowest Fresnel peak strength, and both molecules exhibit a characteristic “bullseye” pattern. This anti-correlation could be explained if the radiolytic processes on Callisto’s trailing hemisphere regolith are depleting the region of its exposed H 2O ice, an ingredient in the generation of CO 2. Alternatively, H 2O ice could be removed from low-latitude regions by sublimation promoted by the highly volatile nature of CO 2, or perhaps is otherwise altered by charged particles that interact with the trailing hemisphere. — We detect a patchy CO 2 atmosphere with peak column densities ∼1.4 × 10 19 m−2 on the Valhalla-centered observation. We find that the peak column densities of the gaseous CO 2 are offset from the region of strongest solid-phase CO 2 absorption. A similar offset was noted for Callisto’s trailing hemisphere (Cartwright et al. 2024). Future modeling efforts as well as telescopic observations are needed to understand the mechanisms driving CO 2 release from the surfaces of icy moons like Callisto. 17 — We find that the band depth of the 4 .57 μm feature is stronger on Callisto’s leading hemisphere, including with the new Valhalla-centered observation, than on the trailing hemisphere. The material responsible for the 4 .57 μmabsorption, which may be a CN-bearing molecule (e.g., Cartwright et al. 2024), and the recent discovery of ammoniated silicates on the Jovian irregular satellites (Sharkey et al. 2025) offers a potential source of Nitrogen for Callisto’s surface. Additional labwork using temperature and radiation conditions appropriate for Callisto is required to unravel the origin of its 4 .57 μm absorption.
